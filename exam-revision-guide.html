The Exam Revision Guide

Why the revision that feels most productive is usually the least effective, and what to do instead.

Almost everyone revises by reading. It is comfortable, it is easy to start, and it produces a steadily growing sense that the material is under control. That sense is the problem.

Recall, not recognition

Reading a page for the fourth time makes it familiar. Familiarity feels like knowledge and behaves nothing like it: in an exam you are not asked to recognise the material, you are asked to produce it. The gap between those two only shows up when the book is shut.

So turn every revision session into a production task. Close the notes and write what you remember. Answer a question without looking. Explain the topic out loud as though someone else were in the room. Then check, and let the gaps decide what you revise next rather than the order of the syllabus.

Feels likeActually doesInstead
Rereading notesBuilds familiarity onlyWrite the page from memory, then compare
HighlightingMarks the text, not the memoryTurn each highlight into a question
Copying out notesOccupies the hand, not the mindCondense into fewer words than you started with
Watching a lecture againPassive, and slower than readingWatch once, then summarise without pausing

Spacing and mixing

Two sessions a week apart beat two sessions in one afternoon, by a wide margin, for the same total time. The small amount of forgetting between them is what makes the second session work. You do not need a complicated schedule to benefit from this; you need to touch each topic more than once, with a gap.

Mixing topics within a session has the same shape: it is harder while you do it, feels less satisfying, and produces better recall weeks later. One subject per day feels smoother and fades faster.

Condensing notes

Revision notes should be shorter than the originals, and then shorter again. The useful version of a topic is one side of paper: the claim, the mechanism, the two examples and the objection. If it does not fit, you have summarised rather than understood.

Write the condensed version from memory, not by copying. The act of deciding what to leave out is where the learning happens, which is why someone else's notes are so much less useful than your own.

Past papers, properly

Do them closed-book and to time, then mark them against the scheme as unkindly as you can manage. An untimed, open-book attempt tells you almost nothing, because the thing being tested is retrieval under pressure.

Pay attention to the command words. “Evaluate” and “describe” want different answers, and marks are routinely lost by writing a good answer to the wrong verb.

The last fortnight

By now, stop covering new ground. Full papers under exam conditions, marking, and targeted work on the topics that came back wrong. Use the Exam Countdown Planner to see how the weeks split, and the Grade Target Calculator if you want to know what the paper actually has to deliver.

Keep one day a week clear. The people who lose the final fortnight are almost always the ones who tried to work every day of the previous six weeks.

The day itself

Read the whole paper first and allocate minutes per mark before you write a word. Answer the question you can answer best second rather than first, so you are warmed up but not short of time. If you run out of time on a question, bullet-point the rest of the argument: markers can award for structure and content, not for sentences you never got to.
